The basic negative is simple: put **no** directly before the verb.

> `**No** hablo español.`
> `**No** he terminado.`

Nothing goes between `no` and the verb except a pronoun: `**No me** gusta`, `**No lo** he visto`.

## The negative words

| | |
|---|---|
| **nada** | nothing |
| **nadie** | nobody |
| **nunca / jamás** | never |
| **ninguno / ningún** | no, not any |
| **tampoco** | neither, not either |
| **ni** | nor, not even |

## Two negatives are correct

This is the rule English speakers resist hardest. If a negative word comes **after** the verb,
Spanish requires `no` in front as well:

> `**No** he hecho **nada**.` — literally "I haven't done nothing"
> `**No** conozco a **nadie**.`
> `**No** viene **nunca**.`

That is not sloppy Spanish — it is the only correct form. What you *cannot* say is
*he hecho nada*.

## Or put the negative first and drop the no

Move the negative word before the verb and `no` disappears:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**No** viene **nunca**.` | = |
| `**Nunca** viene.` | |

Both are correct and mean the same. `Nunca he ido a México` and `No he ido nunca a México` are
interchangeable.

## ningún

`ninguno` shortens to **ningún** before a masculine noun, exactly like `uno` → `un`:

- `**No** tiene **ningún** problema.`
- `**No** tiene **ninguna** cita.`

It is almost always singular, even where English would use a plural: `no tengo ningún amigo
aquí`, not *ningunos amigos*.

## Watch out

**tampoco** is the negative twin of `también`:

- `Yo **también**.` — Me too.
- `Yo **tampoco**.` — Me neither.

Getting these backwards is one of the most audible learner slips, because they come up in every
conversation.
