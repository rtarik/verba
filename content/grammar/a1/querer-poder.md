Three very common verbs that share one convenient habit: put a **plain, unchanged verb** straight
after them.

| | |
|---|---|
| `Quiero **comer**.` | I want to eat. |
| `Puedo **cocinar**.` | I can cook. |
| `Sé **cocinar**.` | I know how to cook. |

The second verb keeps its dictionary form — `comer`, not *como*. English does the same thing
("I want to eat"), minus the "to".

## The forms

| | querer (want) | poder (can) | saber (know how) |
|---|---|---|---|
| yo | qu**ie**ro | p**ue**do | **sé** |
| tú | qu**ie**res | p**ue**des | sabes |
| él / ella | qu**ie**re | p**ue**de | sabe |
| nosotros | queremos | podemos | sabemos |
| ellos | qu**ie**ren | p**ue**den | saben |

`querer` and `poder` are the stem-changing verbs from
[the present tense](present-tense-regular): the middle breaks when stressed, and goes quiet in
the `nosotros` form. `saber` is regular apart from `sé`.

## poder or saber?

Both become "can" in English, and picking the wrong one changes your meaning:

| | |
|---|---|
| `No **sé** cocinar.` | I never learned. A permanent fact about me. |
| `No **puedo** cocinar.` | Something is stopping me right now — no time, no kitchen. |

*No sé cocinar* is an admission. *No puedo cocinar* is an excuse. Ana's text uses the first
deliberately.

## Watch out

`querer` also means "to love" when its object is a person: `Te quiero` is "I love you", not
"I want you". Spanish leans on context here, and gets away with it.
