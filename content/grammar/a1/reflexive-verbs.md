Some Spanish verbs come with an extra little word: `me`, `te`, `se`. It means the action lands
back on the person doing it.

- `levanto` — I raise (something)
- `**me** levanto` — I raise **myself**, i.e. I get up

You met this in Unit 1 with `me llamo` — literally "I call myself".

## The pronouns

| | |
|---|---|
| **me** levanto | I get up |
| **te** levantas | you get up |
| **se** levanta | he / she gets up |
| **nos** levantamos | we get up |
| **se** levantan | they get up |

The pronoun goes **before** the verb, and it changes to match who you are talking about. The
verb ending changes as normal on top of that.

## Why English speakers find this odd

English mostly dropped this. We say "I get up", not "I get myself up"; "I shower", not "I shower
myself". Spanish keeps it, so a set of everyday actions need the pronoun and sound wrong
without it:

| Spanish | Literally | Natural English |
|---|---|---|
| me levanto | I raise myself | I get up |
| me ducho | I shower myself | I shower |
| me acuesto | I lay myself down | I go to bed |

## The part that actually matters

For some verbs, adding the pronoun genuinely changes the meaning — this is not just decoration:

- `duermo` — I sleep
- `**me** duermo` — I fall asleep

Both appear in *Por la noche*, two lines apart. `Duermo ocho horas` is about the sleeping;
`me duermo pronto` is about the moment of dropping off.

## Watch out

The pronoun is not optional and not interchangeable. `Se levanto` is wrong, and `me levanta`
means something quite different — that someone is lifting *me*.
