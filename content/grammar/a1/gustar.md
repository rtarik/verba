`Me gusta el café` does not mean "I like coffee" in the way English builds it. It means
**"coffee pleases me."**

That sounds like a technicality until you try to say "I like oranges" and get it wrong.

## The thing you like is the subject

| Spanish | Literally | English |
|---|---|---|
| Me gust**a** el café. | The coffee pleases me. | I like coffee. |
| Me gust**an** las naranjas. | The oranges please me. | I like oranges. |

The verb agrees with **the food**, not with you. One thing → `gusta`. Several things → `gustan`.
You never say *"yo gusto"* unless you mean that other people find *you* appealing.

## The pronoun says who is pleased

| | |
|---|---|
| **me** gusta | I like |
| **te** gusta | you like |
| **le** gusta | he / she likes |
| **nos** gusta | we like |
| **les** gusta | they like |

These sit in front of the verb and never change form.

## Why Spanish says it twice

You will often see the person named *and* pronouned in the same sentence:

> `**A mi hermana** no **le** gusta el pescado.`

`a mi hermana` and `le` both point at the same person. This is not redundancy to be trimmed —
`le` alone would be ambiguous (him? her?), and `a mi hermana` alone would be ungrammatical. Both
are required.

The same pattern gives you `A mí me gusta` for emphasis, and `¿Y a ti?` for "and you?"

## Watch out

After `a`, the pronouns change shape: it is `a **mí**`, `a **ti**` — never *a yo* or *a tú*.

And when what you like is an action, use the singular:

> `Me gusta cocinar.` — I like cooking.

One activity, so `gusta`, even if you list several: `Me gusta cocinar y comer.`
