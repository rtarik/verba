In Spanish, every noun is either **masculine** or **feminine** — including things with no
natural gender at all. A house is feminine. A cat is masculine. There is no deep logic to it,
and fluent speakers simply learn each noun together with its gender.

## The useful pattern

Most nouns ending in **-o** are masculine, and most ending in **-a** are feminine.

| | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| noun | el gat**o** | la cas**a** |
| "a" | **un** gato | **una** casa |
| "the" | **el** gato | **la** casa |

Learn each new noun with its article attached — `una casa`, not just `casa`. It costs nothing
extra now and saves you from guessing later.

## Adjectives have to match

Once a noun has a gender, the words describing it follow along:

- **Mi casa es pequeña** — the `-a` ending is feminine, because *casa* is feminine
- **Es un gato negro** — the `-o` ending is masculine, because *gato* is masculine

This is why the same English word "small" shows up as *pequeño* or *pequeña* depending on what
it describes.

## Watch out

`ciudad` ends in **-d**, not -a, but it is feminine: `una ciudad grande`. The -o/-a rule is a
strong hint, not a law. Nouns ending in **-dad**, **-ción**, and **-sión** are reliably feminine,
which covers a lot of ground.

Note also that `grande` ends in -e and does not change at all — adjectives ending in -e are the
same for both genders. One less thing to remember.
