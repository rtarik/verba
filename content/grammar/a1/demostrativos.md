Spanish has **three** demonstratives where English has two. The extra one marks distance from
both speakers.

| | near me | near you | far from both |
|---|---|---|---|
| masculine | **este** | **ese** | **aquel** |
| feminine | **esta** | **esa** | **aquella** |
| masc. plural | **estos** | **esos** | **aquellos** |
| fem. plural | **estas** | **esas** | **aquellas** |

- `**Este** libro` — the one in my hand
- `**Ese** libro` — the one near you
- `**Aquel** libro` — the one over there, away from us both

English collapses the last two into "that", which is why learners underuse `aquel`.

## Distance in time, too

The same three-way split works for time, and this is where you meet them most in these texts:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**este** año` | this year, the one we're in |
| `**ese** día` | that day, recently mentioned |
| `**aquella** tarde` | that afternoon, further back |

`Aquella tarde no leí un libro` places the afternoon at a distance — months ago, in a different
chapter of the story.

## The neuter forms

**esto**, **eso**, **aquello** have no gender because they point at an idea rather than a noun:

- `¿Qué es **esto**?` — what is this?
- `**Eso** me ha hecho feliz.` — that made me happy

You cannot say *este me ha hecho feliz* unless you mean a specific masculine thing. When
referring to a whole situation, always the neuter.

## Watch out

Older writing puts accents on the pronouns — `éste`, `ése` — to distinguish them from the
adjectives. The accent was made optional in 1959 and formally dropped in 2010, so modern Spanish
writes them all without. You will still see the accents in books printed before then.
