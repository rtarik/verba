Both can look like "there is" in English, and choosing between them trips up almost every
beginner. The rule is short:

> **hay** introduces something. **está** locates something you have already introduced.

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Hay** un parque cerca.` | There is a park nearby — you did not know it existed |
| `El parque **está** cerca.` | The park is nearby — you know the park; this says where |

## The giveaway is the little word in front

This is the practical test, and it is almost always reliable:

- **hay** is followed by `un`, `una`, `muchos`, a number, or nothing at all
- **está** is followed by `el`, `la`, `mi`, `este` — something already identified

So `Hay un supermercado` and `El supermercado está en mi calle` are both natural. But
`Hay el supermercado` is wrong, and so is `Un supermercado está en mi calle` as an opening
sentence.

## Why it works that way

`hay` answers *what exists here?* — so what follows it is new information, and new things take
`un`/`una`. `está` answers *where is it?* — so what comes before it must be a thing both of you
already have in mind, which is what `el`/`la` signals.

Once you hear it that way, the article does the deciding for you.

## Seen in Unit 3

From *Mi barrio*, one sentence after another:

> `En mi calle **hay** muchas tiendas.` — introducing them
> `El parque **está** lejos.` — you already know which park

## Watch out

`hay` never changes: one word for singular and plural alike. `está` does change — `está` for one
thing, `están` for several:

- `**Hay** un árbol.` / `**Hay** muchos árboles.`
- `El árbol **está** en el jardín.` / `Los árboles **están** en el jardín.`
