**hay** means both "there is" and "there are". One word covers both, and it never changes form.

- `**Hay** un ordenador.` — There is a computer.
- `**Hay** muchos estudiantes.` — There are many students.

English forces you to choose between *is* and *are* depending on what follows. Spanish does not,
which makes this one of the few places where Spanish asks less of you than English.

## What hay is for

Use it to say that something exists, without saying anything more about it:

> `En mi clase **hay** muchos estudiantes.`

## hay vs está

The distinction is worth getting right, because both can look like "there is" in English.

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Hay** un libro en la mesa.` | There is a book on the table — introducing it; you did not know it existed |
| `El libro **está** en la mesa.` | The book is on the table — you already know the book; this says where it is |

The pattern: **hay** introduces something new, **está** locates something already known. Which
is why `hay` is followed by `un`, `dos`, `muchos` — but almost never by `el` or `la`.

## Watch out

`hay` is not a form of `ser` or `estar`, even though all three translate as "to be" in places.
It belongs to a different verb, `haber`, and at this stage `hay` is the only form of it you
need.
