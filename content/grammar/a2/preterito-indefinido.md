This is the tense for things that are **finished and gone**: yesterday, last week, in 1960.
Unlike `he trabajado`, it is a single word.

| | -ar (trabajar) | -er / -ir (comer, vivir) |
|---|---|---|
| yo | trabaj**é** | com**í** |
| tú | trabaj**aste** | com**iste** |
| él / ella | trabaj**ó** | com**ió** |
| nosotros | trabaj**amos** | com**imos** |
| ellos | trabaj**aron** | com**ieron** |

The good news: **-er and -ir verbs share one set of endings**. You only have two patterns to
learn, not three.

## The accents are not decoration

In the `yo` and `él` forms, the accent is the only thing separating tenses:

| | |
|---|---|
| `trabajo` | I work — *today* |
| `trabaj**ó**` | he worked — *yesterday* |
| `trabaj**é**` | I worked — *yesterday* |

Three different meanings, one written syllable apart. Spanish speakers hear the stress
immediately; readers need the accent to know where it falls.

## Two forms that look like the present

`comimos` and `vivimos` are identical in the present and the preterite. So are all `-ir` verbs
in the `nosotros` form. Spanish accepts the ambiguity and lets the sentence settle it:

> `El sábado com**imos** en un restaurante.` — the time marker does the work.

## Spelling changes that keep the sound

A few `yo` forms change their spelling so the consonant keeps sounding the same before `-é`:

- llegar → **llegué** (not *llegé*)
- pagar → **pagué**
- empezar → **empecé**

Nothing has changed about the verb; the spelling is chasing the pronunciation.

## What triggers this tense

Look for a finished time expression: `ayer`, `anoche`, `la semana pasada`, `el año pasado`,
`en 1960`, `hace dos años`. If the period is over, this is your tense — see
[hoy he comido, ayer comí](perfecto-vs-indefinido) for the full contrast.
