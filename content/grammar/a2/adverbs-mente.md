This is the best-value rule in Spanish: every adjective you already know can become an adverb.

> **Take the feminine form of the adjective. Add -mente.**

| adjective | adverb | |
|---|---|---|
| claro → clar**a** | clara**mente** | clearly |
| lento → lent**a** | lenta**mente** | slowly |
| perfecto → perfect**a** | perfecta**mente** | perfectly |
| exacto → exact**a** | exacta**mente** | exactly |

It is the exact counterpart of English "-ly", and it behaves just as predictably.

## Adjectives that don't end in -o

If the adjective has no separate feminine form, just add `-mente` to what is there:

- probable → probable**mente**
- simple → simple**mente**
- normal → normal**mente**
- fácil → fácil**mente**

## The accent survives

If the adjective carries a written accent, it keeps it — even though the stress of the spoken
word has moved:

- último → **ú**ltimamente
- fácil → f**á**cilmente
- rápido → r**á**pidamente

These words end up with two stresses, which is unusual in Spanish and worth noticing.

## Two adverbs in a row

When you use two, only the last one takes `-mente`:

> `Habla lenta y claramente.` — He speaks slowly and clearly.

Not *lentamente y claramente*. The first adjective simply sits there in its feminine form.

## Watch out

Spanish uses these less than English does. Where English piles on "-ly" adverbs, Spanish often
prefers a phrase:

| | |
|---|---|
| `con cuidado` | carefully |
| `sin prisa` | unhurriedly |
| `de repente` | suddenly |
| `por fin` | finally |

`De repente` is far more natural than *repentinamente*. When a `-mente` word feels heavy, there
is usually a shorter phrase doing the same job.
