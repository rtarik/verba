Spanish has two ways of saying "you". English lost this distinction centuries ago, so there is
nothing to map it onto — but the mechanics are simpler than they look.

> **usted takes the he/she form of the verb.**

That is the whole rule.

| tú | usted |
|---|---|
| ¿**Puedes** ayudarme? | ¿**Puede** ayudarme? |
| ¿Cómo **te llamas**? | ¿Cómo **se llama**? |
| **Tienes** tiempo. | **Tiene** tiempo. |

## Everything else follows along

- "your" is **su**, not `tu`: `**Su** pasaporte, por favor.`
- the reflexive is **se**, not `te`: `No **se** preocupe.`
- the object pronoun is **le**, not `te`: `**Le** doy las gracias.`

## The plural: ustedes and vosotros

`usted` pluralises to **ustedes**, taking the they-form.

Spain also has **vosotros**, an informal plural with its own endings:

| | tú | vosotros |
|---|---|---|
| present | habl**as** | habl**áis** |
| | tien**es** | ten**éis** |
| | sab**es** | sab**éis** |
| command | habla | habl**ad** |

`vosotros` is used constantly in Spain and **does not exist in Latin America**, where `ustedes`
covers both formal and informal plural. Latin American speakers understand it perfectly but never
produce it.

For a learner this is genuinely optional. If you learn Spanish for Spain you will need to
recognise `vosotros` from day one and will pick up producing it naturally; if you learn for Latin
America you can ignore it entirely except when reading.

## When to use it

| tú | usted |
|---|---|
| friends, family, colleagues | strangers in official settings |
| anyone your age or younger | much older people |
| most situations in Spain | police, doctors, formal letters |

**Regional caution.** Spain uses `tú` far more widely than learners expect — shop assistants,
waiters and new colleagues will usually `tú` you straight away, and insisting on `usted` can
sound cold or distancing. Much of Latin America is the reverse, and Colombia in particular uses
`usted` even between close friends.

The safe strategy is the same everywhere: **start with `usted` with a stranger, and switch when
they switch**. Spanish speakers often say so explicitly — `podemos tutearnos` — which is an
invitation to relax.

## Watch out

Because `usted` uses he/she forms, sentences can be genuinely ambiguous: `¿Tiene tiempo?` could
mean "do you have time?" or "does he have time?". Context normally resolves it, and adding
`usted` itself removes any doubt: `¿Tiene usted tiempo?`
