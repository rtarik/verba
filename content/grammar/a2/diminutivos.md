Spanish modifies nouns and adjectives with endings that English handles with extra words — and
they carry far more than size.

## The diminutive: -ito / -ita

| | |
|---|---|
| un momento → un **momentito** | just a moment |
| ahora → **ahorita** | in a little while |
| poco → un **poquito** | a tiny bit |
| casa → **casita** | a little house (fondly) |
| abuela → **abuelita** | granny |

Size is often the *least* of it. The main jobs are:

- **affection** — `abuelita`, `cariñito`
- **softening a request** — `un momentito`, `una preguntita` are markedly gentler
- **making something less serious** — `un problemita`
- occasionally **condescension**, depending entirely on tone

Spelling adjusts to keep the sound: `poco` → `poqu**ito**`, `amigo` → `amigu**ito**`. Words
ending in `-n`, `-r` or `-e` often take `-cito`: `joven → jovencito`, `café → cafecito`.

## Regional weight

Diminutives are far more frequent in Latin America than in Spain, and in some countries —
Mexico, Colombia, Costa Rica — they are constant. `Ahorita` is the clearest case: in Mexico it
can mean now, in ten minutes, or never.

## The augmentative: -ísimo

Attached to adjectives, it means *very*, and is one of the most useful endings in the language:

- grande → **grandísimo**
- bueno → **buenísimo**
- caro → **carísimo**
- aburrido → **aburridísimo**

The stem may adjust: `rico → riquísimo`, `largo → larguísimo`, `feliz → felicísimo`.

## -ón and -azo

`-ón` enlarges, often unflatteringly: `casa → casón`, `soltero → solterón`. `-azo` can mean a
blow — `un codazo` is an elbow to the ribs — or admiration: `un cochazo` is a magnificent car.

## Watch out

Diminutives are hard to use well early on. They are easy to *understand* and easy to
**overuse** — a learner who has just discovered `-ito` tends to attach it to everything, which
reads as either childish or sarcastic. Recognise them first; produce them once you have heard
where they land.
