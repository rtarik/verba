Both often become "for" in English, which is why they get confused. The distinction in Spanish is
sharper than in English:

> **por** looks backwards — the cause. **para** looks forwards — the purpose.

| | |
|---|---|
| `Estudio **por** Pablo.` | because of Pablo — he is the reason |
| `Estudio **para** viajar.` | in order to travel — that is the goal |

A useful pair to hold on to: **por qué** (why) asks about cause; **para qué** (what for) asks
about purpose.

## What each one covers

**por**

- cause: `Estudio **por** gusto.`
- movement through: `Paseo **por** el parque.`
- vague time: `**por** la tarde`, `**por** la mañana`
- exchange: `Pagué veinte euros **por** el libro.`
- per: `dos veces **por** semana`

**para**

- purpose: `Estudio **para** entender las canciones.`
- recipient: `Este libro es **para** ti.`
- destination: `Salgo **para** Madrid.`
- deadline: `Lo necesito **para** el lunes.`
- opinion: `**Para** mí, es difícil.`

## The one that catches everyone

Compare these two, which are both correct and mean different things:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Lo hago **por** ti.` | I do it because of you — for your sake, you are the reason |
| `Lo hago **para** ti.` | I do it for you — you will receive the result |

If you buy a present, it is `para ti`. If you gave up smoking because of your family, it is
`por tu familia`.

## Watch out

`para` before a verb means "in order to", and Spanish uses it far more readily than English uses
"in order to". Where English drops it — *"I study to travel"* — Spanish keeps it:
`Estudio **para** viajar`. Leaving it out is a common beginner slip.
