Three ways of saying how long something has been going on. All three appear constantly, and
none of them maps neatly onto English.

## hace + time + que + present

> `**Hace** tres meses **que** estudio español.`
> I have been studying Spanish for three months.

Note the tense: Spanish uses the **present**, because the studying is still happening. English
insists on a perfect ("I have been studying"), which is why learners write *he estudiado hace
tres meses* — and that means something different.

The same words in the other order drop the `que`:

> `Estudio español **desde hace** tres meses.`

## hace + time on its own = ago

Without `que`, and with a past tense, `hace` means **ago**:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Hace** tres meses **que** estudio.` | I've been studying for three months |
| `Empecé **hace** tres meses.` | I started three months ago |

One is ongoing, the other finished. The tense of the main verb tells you which.

## llevar + time

The most idiomatic of the three, and the one with no English equivalent at all:

- `**Llevo** dos años estudiando.` — I've been studying for two years
- `**Llevamos** dos meses sin agua.` — we've gone two months without water
- `Los carteles **llevan** meses en las ventanas.` — the signs have been there months

The pattern is `llevar` + time + (gerund / `sin` + infinitive / a place). It is extremely common
in speech and worth using rather than just recognising.

## desde and desde que

- **desde** + a point in time: `**desde** septiembre`, `**desde** 2019`
- **desde que** + a clause: `**desde que** te conozco`

Compare with `desde hace` + a length of time. The distinction is point versus duration:
`desde marzo` (since March) against `desde hace tres meses` (for three months).

## Watch out

`llevar` has several unrelated jobs — to carry, to wear, to take someone somewhere. Only with a
time expression does it mean "to have been". Context makes it obvious, but the word itself gives
no clue.
