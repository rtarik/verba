Two things you already know, combined: the **subjunctive of `haber`** plus a participle.

| | |
|---|---|
| yo | **haya** llegado |
| tú | **hayas** llegado |
| él / ella | **haya** llegado |
| nosotros | **hayamos** llegado |
| ellos | **hayan** llegado |

`haya` is one of the six irregular subjunctives from Unit 11. The participles are the same ones
from Unit 5. There is genuinely nothing new here except the combination.

## When to use it

Whenever a subjunctive trigger points at something **already finished**:

| trigger | |
|---|---|
| wish | `Espero que **haya llegado** bien.` |
| doubt | `No creo que **haya llamado**.` |
| emotion | `Me alegro de que **hayas venido**.` |
| `ojalá` | `Ojalá no se **haya enfadado**.` |

Compare the two tenses in the same frame:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Espero que **llegue** bien.` | the journey is still ahead |
| `Espero que **haya llegado** bien.` | the journey is over; I'm hoping about the outcome |

## The English equivalent barely exists

English collapses this: *"I hope she arrived safely"* and *"I hope she arrives safely"* differ
only by tense, with no mood change at all. That is why learners produce
*espero que llegó* — importing the English indicative. Spanish will not allow it: after `espero
que`, the verb must be subjunctive, and the only question is which one.

## Watch out

`haya` and `halla` sound identical in most of the Spanish-speaking world and mean completely
different things — `haya` is this auxiliary, `halla` is a form of `hallar` (to find). Spelling
them apart is a common native-speaker error too.

There is also a past version, `hubiera llegado`, for wishes and doubts about a more distant past.
That belongs with the third conditional in Unit 20.
