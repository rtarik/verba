When the direct object of a verb is a **person**, Spanish puts `a` in front of it.

| | |
|---|---|
| `Veo **la casa**.` | a thing — no `a` |
| `Veo **a mi hermana**.` | a person — `a` required |

It is not a preposition of place or direction. It means nothing at all. Its only job is to mark
that what follows is somebody rather than something.

You have been reading it since Unit 7 — `conocí **a** Sofía`, `ayudé **a** mi hermana` — and it
is not optional.

## Why Spanish needs it

Because word order is flexible. Spanish can put the object first without changing the meaning,
and without the `a` the sentence would be ambiguous:

> `El perro mordió **al** niño.`
> `**Al** niño lo mordió el perro.`

Both say the dog did the biting. Take the `a` away and there is nothing left to tell you who bit
whom. English relies on word order for this; Spanish relies on the marker, which is what buys it
the freedom to move things around.

## Where it applies

- **People**: `Llamé **a** Diego.` `Busco **a** mi profesora.`
- **Pets and loved animals**: `Quiero **a** mi gato.` — but `Quiero un gato` (any cat) has none.
- **Personified things**: `Teme **a** la muerte.`

## Where it does not

- **Things**: `Llamé **al** banco.` — that `al` is the ordinary preposition plus `el`, not this one.
- **Unspecified people**: `Busco **un** médico.` — any doctor will do. Compare `Busco **al**
  médico` — a particular one.
- **After `tener`**: `Tengo dos hermanas.` — never `tengo a dos hermanas`.

That `tener` exception is worth remembering, because it breaks the rule for one of the commonest
verbs in the language.

## Watch out

The contraction still applies: `a` + `el` = **al**. `Vi **al** profesor`, never *a el profesor*.
