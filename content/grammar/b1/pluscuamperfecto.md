When two things happened in the past and you need to make clear **which came first**, Spanish
puts the earlier one in the pluscuamperfecto.

> `Cuando llegué, ya **se había ido**.`

Both events are past. The leaving happened before the arriving, and `había ido` says so.

## How to build it

The **imperfecto of `haber`** plus a participle. You have both halves already.

| | |
|---|---|
| yo | **había** hablado |
| tú | **habías** hablado |
| él / ella | **había** hablado |
| nosotros | **habíamos** hablado |
| ellos | **habían** hablado |

The participles are the same ones from Unit 5, irregulars included: `hecho`, `dicho`, `visto`,
`escrito`, `abierto`, `vuelto`, `puesto`.

And as always, nothing may come between the two words: `**Nunca había** visto`, not
*había nunca visto*.

## Where it shows up

**Stacking background before a story:**

> `**Había estudiado** seis meses. **Había aprendido** cientos de palabras. **Había hecho** todos
> los ejercicios. Y aun así no entendí nada.`

Three layers of preparation, then the event that undoes them.

**With ya and nunca**, which are its natural companions:

- `Ya **había comprado** dos libros.`
- `Nunca **había visto** a Diego puntual.`

## It also works with the gerund

`había estado escuchando` — I had been listening. The pluscuamperfecto of `estar` plus a gerund,
combining two constructions you already know.

## Watch out

English is much looser here. We often say "when I arrived he already left", letting *already* do
the work. Spanish will not: if one past event precedes another, the earlier one takes `había`.
Using the plain preterite instead — `cuando llegué, ya se fue` — sounds wrong rather than merely
informal.
