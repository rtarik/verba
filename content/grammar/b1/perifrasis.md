Spanish builds a great deal of everyday meaning by putting two verbs together. You already know
two of these — `ir a` + infinitive and `estar` + gerund. Here are five more, and between them
they cover an enormous amount of ordinary speech.

| structure | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| **acabar de** + inf. | to have just | `Acabo de llegar.` |
| **seguir** + gerund | to still be doing | `Sigo estudiando.` |
| **volver a** + inf. | to do again | `Volví a leerlo.` |
| **dejar de** + inf. | to stop doing | `Dejé de usar el diccionario.` |
| **ponerse a** + inf. | to start suddenly | `Me puse a llorar.` |

Only the first verb conjugates. The second stays as an infinitive or gerund, always.

## Two that English has no single word for

**acabar de** is the only way to say "to have just". There is no alternative construction:
`Acabo de escribirlo` — I've just written it.

**volver a** replaces English "again" entirely. Spanish prefers `volví a leerlo` to
*lo leí otra vez*, and it sounds more natural.

## Which take a gerund

Only `seguir` and `continuar` take the gerund. `Sigo estudiar` is wrong; it must be
`sigo estudiando`. The others all take an infinitive.

## Watch out for dejar

`dejar` has three jobs and they are easy to confuse:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Dejé de** fumar.` | I stopped smoking |
| `**Dejé** el libro en la mesa.` | I left the book on the table |
| `Me **dejó** entrar.` | he let me in |

Only the first is a verb chain. The `de` is what makes it one.

## They stack

These combine freely, which is where the economy comes from:

> `**Sigo sin** entender.` — I still don't understand.
> `**Acabo de dejar de** fumar.` — I've just stopped smoking.
> `**Volví a ponerme a** estudiar.` — I started studying again.

Few pieces, many combinations.
