Disagreement runs on set phrases, and knowing them is the difference between sounding blunt and
sounding like a participant.

## Agreeing

| | |
|---|---|
| **Estoy de acuerdo (contigo).** | I agree (with you) |
| **Tienes razón.** | You're right |
| **Exacto. / Eso es.** | Exactly |
| **Yo también lo veo así.** | I see it that way too |

Note it is `estar **de** acuerdo`. `Estoy acuerdo` is not Spanish.

## Disagreeing

| | |
|---|---|
| **No estoy de acuerdo.** | I don't agree |
| **No lo veo así.** | I don't see it that way |
| **Yo diría más bien que...** | I'd say rather that... |
| **En absoluto.** | Absolutely not |

`en absoluto` looks like emphatic agreement and means the opposite: *not at all*. It is one of
the easiest words in Spanish to get backwards.

## Softening

Spanish disagreement usually arrives wrapped:

> `**Tienes razón, pero** no es tan sencillo.`
> `**Depende.**`
> `**Entiendo lo que dices, aunque** yo lo veo distinto.`

`No lo veo así` is the most useful of these: it sounds polite and concedes nothing at all.

## Half-agreeing

**Ya, bueno** is worth knowing because you will hear it constantly. Formally it agrees. In
practice it means *I am not going to keep discussing this*, and the conversation moves on.

`Ya` on its own works the same way — a listener saying `ya, ya` is signalling that they have
heard enough, not that they are enthusiastic.

## Watch out

Remember the mood rule from Unit 12. Asserting takes the indicative; denying takes the
subjunctive:

- `Creo que **tiene** razón.`
- `No creo que **tenga** razón.`

Disagreement is where that pattern earns its keep, because it comes up in every second sentence
of an argument.
