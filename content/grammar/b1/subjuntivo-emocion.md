The third trigger, and the one that most confuses learners — because here **the fact is not in
doubt at all**.

> `Me alegro de que **esté** en casa.`

She really is at home. Nobody is questioning it. The subjunctive appears because the sentence is
a *reaction* to the fact rather than a statement of it.

That is the key idea: Spanish marks the difference between **reporting** the world and
**responding** to it.

## The expressions

| | |
|---|---|
| **alegrarse de que** | `Me alegro de que estés bien.` |
| **sentir que** | `Siento que tenga que aguantarla.` |
| **molestar que** | `Me molesta que llegue tarde.` |
| **sorprender que** | `Me sorprende que tenga tanta energía.` |
| **dar rabia / pena que** | `Me da rabia que no descanse.` |
| **gustar que** | `Me gusta que vengas.` |
| **temer que** | `Temo que sea tarde.` |

Many of these behave like `gustar` — the thing that pleases or annoys is the subject, and you are
the indirect object. Unit 4's pattern, extended.

## The de que

Emotion verbs built with a preposition keep it before `que`: `alegrarse **de que**`,
`estar contento **de que**`. Dropping the `de` is extremely common in speech and is called
*queísmo*; it is understood everywhere but avoided in writing.

The reverse mistake — adding `de` where it does not belong (*pienso de que*) — is *dequeísmo*, and
that one is stigmatised in speech too.

## Same subject, no subjunctive

The Unit 11 rule still holds. One person means an infinitive:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Me alegro de **estar** aquí.` | I'm glad **I'm** here |
| `Me alegro de que **estés** aquí.` | I'm glad **you're** here |

`Siento **molestarte**` — I'm sorry to bother you. `Siento que **te molestes**` — I'm sorry you're
getting annoyed.

## Watch out

`sentir` means two different things depending on what follows. With `que` + subjunctive it means
**to regret**: `Siento que no puedas venir` is "I'm sorry you can't come", not "I feel that you
can't come". For feeling or perceiving, Spanish would use `notar` or `parecer`.
