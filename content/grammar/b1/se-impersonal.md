Spanish has a way of describing an action without naming anyone at all. English needs "one",
"you", "people", "they", or a passive; Spanish just adds **se**.

> `Aquí **se habla** español.` — Spanish is spoken here.
> `**Se come** bien en Madrid.` — the food is good in Madrid.
> `**Se tarda** veinte minutos.` — it takes twenty minutes.

## Two versions, one word

**Impersonal se** — no subject at all, verb always singular:

- `**Se dice** que va a subir el alquiler.`
- `Eso no **se hace**.`
- `**Se trabaja** demasiado aquí.`

**Passive se** — there *is* a subject, and the verb agrees with it:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Se vende** un piso.` | one flat |
| `**Se venden** tres pisos.` | three flats |
| `No **se admiten** animales.` | plural, so `admiten` |

The distinction rarely matters in practice: if a thing is named, make the verb agree with it; if
nothing is named, keep it singular.

## Where you will actually see it

Signs and notices are almost entirely built from it — `se vende`, `se alquila`, `se traspasa`,
`se busca`, `se ruega silencio`. So are recipes, instructions and rules.

## Why it is so useful

It lets you state something without owning it:

> `**Se dice que** el alquiler subió un cuarenta por ciento.`

Who says so? Nobody in particular. English gets there with "they say" or "apparently", but
Spanish makes it a grammatical construction rather than a hedge — and speakers use it constantly,
for gossip, for rules, and for opinions they would rather not sign.

## Watch out

This `se` is unrelated to the reflexive `se` in `se llama` and to the `se` that replaces `le` in
`se lo di` (Unit 10). Spanish reuses this one small word for at least three different jobs, and
context is the only thing separating them:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Se** llama Ana.` | reflexive — she calls herself |
| `**Se** lo di.` | indirect object — I gave it to them |
| `**Se** habla español.` | impersonal — Spanish is spoken |
