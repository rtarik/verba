Spanish has several ways of saying you got something wrong, and they differ in tone rather than
meaning.

## ojalá + pluperfect subjunctive

The purest form. A wish about something beyond remedy:

> `**Ojalá** le **hubiera preguntado**.` — I wish I'd asked him.

Compare the whole `ojalá` range, which now spans three degrees of impossibility:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Ojalá **venga**.` | a live hope |
| `Ojalá **viniera**.` | unlikely, but not impossible |
| `Ojalá **hubiera venido**.` | closed; it did not happen |

## deber and tener que in the conditional

Drier and more self-accusing:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Debería haber** preguntado.` | I should have asked |
| `**Tendría que haber** ido.` | I ought to have gone |
| `**Podría haber** llamado.` | I could have called |

The pattern is *conditional of the modal* + **haber** + participle. `Debería haber` is the
everyday one; `tendría que haber` sounds more like an obligation you failed.

Note it is `deber**ía** haber`, not *debería de haber* — `deber de` means "must be" as a guess,
which is a different verb entirely.

## The third conditional itself

Often the regret is implied by the structure alone:

> `**Si hubiera sabido**, **habría insistido** más.`

No word for regret appears anywhere; the tenses carry it.

## Watch out

`arrepentirse de` is the literal verb for regretting, and it is stronger than English "regret" —
closer to repenting. `Me arrepiento de haberlo dicho` is a real admission of fault. For everyday
mild regret, Spanish prefers the constructions above, or simply `qué pena que...`.
