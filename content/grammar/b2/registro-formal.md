Formal Spanish is not just longer words. Three things change systematically.

## 1. Address moves to usted

Everything that follows from Unit 10: `puede` not `puedes`, `su` not `tu`, `le` not `te`.

## 2. Requests become indirect

The more indirect the request, the more polite it reads:

| blunt | polite | formal |
|---|---|---|
| `¿Me pasas el informe?` | `¿Puede pasarme el informe?` | `¿Podría enviarme el informe?` |
| `Quiero` | `Querría` | `Quisiera` |
| `Necesito` | `Necesitaría` | `Le agradecería que me facilitara` |

The mechanism is the one from Unit 18: **move away from the present.** Conditional is polite;
imperfect subjunctive is more polite still.

The underlying logic is worth knowing — an indirect request is easier to refuse. Politeness here
is a way of protecting the other person's ability to say no.

## 3. The subject disappears

Formal and administrative Spanish leans heavily on the impersonal `se`:

- `**Se ruega** mantener el silencio.`
- `**Se recuerda** que está prohibido fumar.`
- `**Se procederá** a la revisión.`

Nobody requests, nobody reminds, nobody proceeds. The rule stands on its own, which makes it hard
to argue with — a device of distance as much as of courtesy.

## Where the line falls

The same construction reads very differently depending on context. `Se ruega` on a sign is
neutral. In an email to a colleague it would sound cold or sarcastic.

Spanish workplaces are generally **less formal than the textbooks suggest**, especially in Spain.
Colleagues use `tú` almost immediately. Reserve full formal register for clients, institutions and
people you have never met.

## Watch out

Over-formality is as marked as under-formality. Writing `Estimado señor Ruiz: Quedo a la espera
de su respuesta` to someone who signs off `un abrazo` reads as cold, or as though you are annoyed
with them.
