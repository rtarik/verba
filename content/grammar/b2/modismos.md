Idioms cannot be deduced. You either know them or you don't, and no amount of grammar helps.

## A starting set

| | |
|---|---|
| **estar en las nubes** | to have your head in the clouds |
| **ser pan comido** | to be a piece of cake |
| **tomar el pelo** | to pull someone's leg |
| **no tener pelos en la lengua** | to speak your mind |
| **costar un ojo de la cara** | to cost a fortune |
| **dar en el clavo** | to hit the nail on the head |
| **estar de mala leche** | to be in a foul mood |
| **ponerse las pilas** | to get your act together |
| **no pegar ojo** | not to sleep a wink |
| **echar una mano** | to give a hand |

## The false-friend trap

Many have an English counterpart that resembles them **just enough to mislead**. Spanish does
break the ice — `romper el hielo` — but does not "spill the beans" or "bite the bullet" in any
recognisable way.

The safe assumption is that an English idiom translated literally will be wrong, and often
comically so.

## How they are actually learned

Not from lists. From meeting the same expression several times in context until it stops needing
translation. Twenty learned in an afternoon become two by the evening — which is not a failure
of memory but how idiom acquisition works.

## The risk of using them

An idiom used slightly wrong — wrong register, wrong situation, wrong frequency — sounds like a
costume. Native speakers deploy perhaps a handful in a conversation; a learner who has just
learned ten tends to use all ten.

Hear it three times before you say it once.

## The ones that actually matter

The high-value expressions are not the colourful ones but the invisible ones:

| | |
|---|---|
| **me suena** | it rings a bell |
| **ni idea** | no idea |
| **da igual** | it doesn't matter |
| **vale** | OK |
| **o sea** | I mean |

These appear a hundred times a day and carry almost no imagery at all.
