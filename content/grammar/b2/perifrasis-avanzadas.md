Unit 15 covered the five everyday chains. These are the next tier — less essential, but they are
what makes writing sound Spanish rather than translated.

| | |
|---|---|
| **acabar por** + inf. | to end up doing, after resisting |
| **llegar a** + inf. | to reach the point of |
| **venir a** + inf. | approximately, roughly |
| **dar por** + participle | to consider something settled |
| **pasar a** + inf. | to move on to |
| **ponerse a** + inf. | to start abruptly |
| **echarse a** + inf. | to burst into (llorar, reír) |
| **quedar en** + inf. | to agree to |

## The ones worth learning first

**acabar por** — `Acabé por aceptar.` I ended up accepting, having resisted. Note it is not
`acabar de`, which means to have just done something.

**llegar a** — `Llegué a entender.` Marks that a difficult point was reached. `Llegó a decirlo`
is stronger than `lo dijo` — it implies he went that far.

**dar por** — `Doy por hecho que vendrás.` I take it as given. `Lo dieron por perdido.` They
wrote it off.

## Why bother

Every one of these can be avoided. `Acabé por aceptar` is `al final acepté`; `llegué a entender`
is `por fin entendí`.

But the paraphrase costs more words and reads as translated. English carries these meanings in
adverbs — *eventually, actually, even, roughly* — while Spanish carries them in the verb. Writing
Spanish with English adverbs is grammatical and audibly foreign.

## Watch out

`venir a` is easy to misread. `Viene a costar treinta euros` means *it costs roughly thirty* —
nothing to do with coming. Meanwhile `venir + gerundio` is different again: `viene diciendo lo
mismo desde marzo` means he has been saying it repeatedly since March.
