A group of common adjectives mean genuinely different things depending on which verb they take.

| adjective | with **ser** | with **estar** |
|---|---|---|
| listo | clever | ready |
| aburrido | boring (a bore) | bored |
| rico | wealthy | tasty |
| malo | bad, wicked | ill / gone off |
| bueno | good, kind | tasty / attractive |
| verde | green (colour) | unripe / inexperienced |
| vivo | sharp, lively | alive |
| orgulloso | proud (arrogant) | proud (of something) |
| despierto | bright, alert | awake |

The underlying logic is the Unit 1 rule intact: **ser defines, estar describes a state.** The
difference is that when the definition and the state diverge far enough, English needs two
separate words.

`Es aburrido` says what someone is like. `Está aburrido` says how they feel this afternoon.

## With nationalities and roles

`estar` + a nationality describes behaviour rather than origin:

> `Estás muy español últimamente.`

Not a statement about your passport — a comment on how you have been acting or speaking. The same
works with `estar de`: `está de profesor` means he is working as a teacher for now, as opposed to
`es profesor`, which is what he is.

## With prices and states of affairs

- `¿A cuánto **está** el pan?` — what's bread going for today (it fluctuates)
- `El pan **es** caro.` — bread is expensive as a rule

## Watch out

`ser bueno` and `estar bueno` are worth care. `Es buena` means she is a good person; `está buena`
is a comment on physical appearance and can be crude depending on context. This is the pair most
likely to cause genuine embarrassment, and it is worth knowing before you compliment someone's
cooking.
