At A2 the imperfect/preterite choice is a rule. In narrative writing it becomes an instrument,
and noticing that changes how you read.

## The two jobs

| | |
|---|---|
| **imperfecto** | holds a scene open — atmosphere, description, things in progress |
| **indefinido** | moves the story forward — events, one after another |

> `**Hacía** frío. La casa **estaba** vacía. Nadie **hablaba**.`
> `Entonces **llamaron** a la puerta.`

Three imperfects suspend the reader with nothing happening. One preterite starts the plot. A
writer can hold that suspension for a paragraph or a chapter.

## What this looks like at scale

**Imperfect-heavy prose** feels like memory or dream — nothing quite lands, time doesn't advance.
Some novels are written almost entirely this way.

**Preterite-heavy prose** moves fast and breathes little: *this happened, then this, then this*.
Detective fiction leans on it heavily.

Neither is better. The proportion is a decision about pace, and once you can see it you cannot
unsee it.

## Reading for it

A useful exercise: take any page of a Spanish novel and mark the imperfects and preterites in
different colours. The shape of the page tells you what the author is doing — where they are
setting up, where they are moving.

This is also the fastest way to internalise the distinction, because you meet hundreds of
examples in context rather than a rule with two.

## Watch out

Aspect is not tense. Both tenses are past; they differ in how the action is *viewed*, not when it
happened. English marks this only patchily ("was raining" vs "rained"), which is why the
distinction has to be learned rather than translated.
