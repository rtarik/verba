The conjunctions from Unit 13 behave exactly as they did — they simply move a tense back.

## The ones that always take the subjunctive

`antes de que`, `para que`, `sin que`, `a menos que`, `con tal de que`:

| present | past |
|---|---|
| `antes de que se **vaya**` | `antes de que se **fuera**` |
| `para que **venga**` | `para que **viniera**` |
| `sin que lo **sepa**` | `sin que lo **supiera**` |

No decision to make. If the main verb is past, the subjunctive is imperfect.

## The ones that take either

`cuando`, `en cuanto`, `hasta que`, `mientras`, `después de que` — the mood still depends on
whether the event had happened:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**cuando llegaba**` | a habit that really occurred — indicative |
| `**cuando llegara**` | still ahead at the time — subjunctive |

## A future inside a past

That second case is the one worth slowing down for:

> `Me dijo que me llamaría **cuando llegara**.`

At the moment he spoke, the arriving had not happened. So it is future *relative to a past* — and
Spanish marks it with the imperfect subjunctive. English simply says "when he arrived" and leaves
you to work it out.

It sounds like a contradiction and it is completely ordinary. You will meet it in every narrative
you read.

## aunque, still both ways

The Unit 13 distinction survives the shift intact:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Aunque **llovía**, salimos.` | it really was raining — a fact conceded |
| `Aunque **lloviera**, habríamos salido.` | hypothetical — even if it had rained |

## Watch out

`después de que` is unstable even among native speakers. Strictly it takes the indicative for
real past events — `después de que **llegó**` — but the subjunctive is extremely widespread and
now generally accepted. Both will be understood; neither will be marked wrong.
