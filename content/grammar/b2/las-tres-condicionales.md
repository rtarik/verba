The whole system on one page. The structure is identical in all three; only the tenses move.

| | si half | main half | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st** | present indicative | present / future | `Si tengo tiempo, voy.` |
| **2nd** | imperfect subjunctive | conditional | `Si tuviera tiempo, iría.` |
| **3rd** | pluperfect subjunctive | conditional perfect | `Si hubiera tenido tiempo, habría ido.` |

The first is a **possibility**. The second is a **hypothesis**. The third is a **lament**.

What is elegant about it is that Spanish puts the entire distance between the real and the
impossible inside the conjugation, without adding a single extra word. English needs "if I had"
versus "if I'd had" and leans on context; Spanish makes it structural.

## The one rule shared by all three

**Never `si` + future, and never `si` + conditional.**

- *si aprobaré* ✗
- *si aprobaría* ✗
- *si habría estudiado* ✗

The `si` half takes the present indicative or a past subjunctive. Nothing else.

## Mixed conditionals

The halves can come from different rows, and in real speech they constantly do:

| | |
|---|---|
| `Si **hubiera estudiado** más, ahora **hablaría** mejor.` | impossible past → present consequence |
| `Si **fuera** más organizada, **habría terminado** ayer.` | ongoing trait → past consequence |

The first type is extremely common and rarely taught. Use it whenever a closed past still has
live effects — which is most of the time, since that is what regret actually is.

The test is simple: ask when each half happens, and choose the tense for each half separately.

## Watch out

English collapses the second and third conditionals in speech — "if I knew, I'd have told you"
gets said constantly. Spanish keeps them distinct, and mixing them carelessly changes the
meaning rather than sounding casual.
