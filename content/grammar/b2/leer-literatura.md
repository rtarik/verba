Reading literature is a different skill from reading textbooks, and the difference is register
rather than difficulty.

## A novel holds several registers at once

A single chapter can contain a narrator using the preterite anterior and characters saying
`qué va` and `venga ya`. That distance is deliberate — the gap between how an author narrates and
how their characters speak is one of the oldest tools in fiction.

Learners often read everything **at the same volume**, missing that a character is being formal,
rude, evasive or pompous. Noticing register inside fiction is a whole layer of meaning.

Signals worth watching for:

| | |
|---|---|
| `usted` between characters | distance, hostility, or period setting |
| a sudden `vale`, `oye`, `venga` | the narrator has stepped aside; someone is speaking |
| literary tenses | narration, not dialogue |
| diminutives (`-ito`) | affection, condescension, or regional voice |

## Not understanding everything

In any novel there are words that appear once and never return. Looking up all of them is the
most reliable way to finish no book at all.

A workable method:

1. Underline and carry on.
2. At the end of the chapter, look up anything that appeared **three or more times**.
3. Let the rest go.

Words that matter repeat. Words that don't, don't.

## What to read first

Contemporary novels with a lot of dialogue are easier than classics, because dialogue is closer
to the Spanish you have been learning. Short stories are better than novels at the beginning —
finishing something matters more than its length.

Translations into Spanish of books you already know are the gentlest possible entry: you have the
plot, so you can spend everything on the language.

## Watch out

The urge to understand every sentence is the main thing that stops people reading in a second
language. Tolerating a fog of partial comprehension is a skill, and it is the one that turns
study into reading.
