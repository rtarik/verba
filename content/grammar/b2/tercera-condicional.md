For a past that did not happen, and its consequences that therefore did not either.

> **si** + pluperfect subjunctive → conditional perfect

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Si hubiera estudiado** más, | **habría aprobado**.` |
| `**Si no hubiera conocido** a Pablo, | no **habría empezado** nunca.` |

Both halves are past. Both are untrue. The `si` half takes `hubiera`; the other takes `habría`.

## Never the other way round

As with the second conditional, the subjunctive belongs in the `si` half and nowhere else:

- `Si hubiera estudiado, habría aprobado.` ✓
- *Si habría estudiado, hubiera aprobado.* ✗

`si` + conditional is impossible in Spanish, in any of the three conditionals. If you remember
one thing from this page, that is the one.

## The colloquial version

Very often, spoken Spanish uses `hubiera` in **both** halves:

> `Si hubiera sabido, **hubiera** venido.`

This is entirely normal in speech and in dialogue in fiction. In exams, formal writing and
business correspondence, use `habría` in the second half.

The reverse — `habría` in the `si` half — is never acceptable anywhere.

## Why it feels harder than it is

Every piece is already familiar. `hubiera` is the Unit 18 imperfect subjunctive of `haber`;
`habría` is the Unit 14 conditional perfect; the participles are from Unit 5. The difficulty is
not the forms but the length: two compound verbs in one sentence, six words before you reach the
meaning.

Fluency here comes from repetition rather than analysis.

## Watch out

There is no third conditional without a past. `Si hubiera tenido tiempo, iría` mixes an impossible
past with a present consequence — which is legitimate, and covered in
[the three conditionals](las-tres-condicionales), but it is a *mixed* conditional, not this one.
