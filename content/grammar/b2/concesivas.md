Conceding half of someone's argument before disagreeing is the basic move of Spanish debate.
Four constructions do it, differing mainly in register.

| | |
|---|---|
| **aunque** | all-purpose, spoken and written |
| **a pesar de (que)** | slightly more formal |
| **pese a (que)** | compact, mostly written |
| **si bien** | literary only |

## The mood choice carries over

`aunque`, `a pesar de que` and `pese a que` all take **either mood**, with the Unit 13
distinction intact:

| | |
|---|---|
| `**Aunque llueve**, salimos.` | it is raining — a fact conceded |
| `**Aunque llueva**, saldremos.` | it might rain — a possibility allowed for |

Same with the others: `a pesar de que **es** mejor` (it is) against `a pesar de que **sea**
mejor` (granting it might be).

## What follows them

- **aunque** + a verb
- **a pesar de** + noun or infinitive; **a pesar de que** + verb
- **pese a** + noun; **pese a que** + verb

So `a pesar del sueldo`, `a pesar de aceptarlo`, `a pesar de que lo acepta` — all correct,
different structures.

## si bien

Purely written, and common in journalism and essays: `**Si bien** el sueldo es mejor, el horario
empeora.` It takes the indicative only. You will read it constantly and hear it almost never.

## The rhetorical shape

Concession is rarely neutral. In practice it sets up a reversal:

> **concede** → `Tienes razón en que el sueldo importa.`
> **pivot** → `Ahora bien, no es lo único.`
> **close** → `Yo, en tu lugar, esperaría.`

Conceding more than necessary disarms the other person, which is why it is a strategy rather
than a courtesy. See [structuring an argument](argumentar).

## Watch out

`A pesar de` is followed by `de` — `a pesar **de** que`, not *a pesar que*. Dropping it is a
frequent error among native speakers too, but it is still non-standard.
